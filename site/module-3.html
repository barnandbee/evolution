Our species and our relatives

Homo sapiens emerged in Africa about 300,000 years ago into a world shared with other kinds of humans. Ancient DNA has shown that we met and had children with some of them, and that their genes are still in us. Some of the strongest evidence for that encounter, and for early symbolic thinking, comes from our own region.

3.1 An African origin

Two main models dominated debate in the late 20th century:

  • Multiregional evolution. Modern humans evolved in parallel from H. erectus populations in Africa, Europe and Asia, kept connected by gene flow. Indonesian H. erectus was argued to be ancestral to First Nations Australians. This idea is now rejected.
  • Recent African origin ("Out of Africa"). Homo sapiens evolved in Africa and later spread worldwide, replacing other human populations.

Evidence from fossils and genetics strongly supports the African origin, with an important modification covered below: limited interbreeding occurred with the populations our ancestors met.

The fossil evidence:

  • Jebel Irhoud, Morocco: about 315,000 years ago. These are the oldest fossils assigned to H. sapiens. The face and teeth are close to ours, while the braincase is longer and lower. This suggests the modern face evolved before the modern, rounded skull shape (Hublin et al., 2017).
  • Florisbad, South Africa (about 260,000 years ago); Omo Kibish, Ethiopia (at least 233,000 years ago); Herto, Ethiopia (about 160,000 years ago) (Vidal et al., 2022).

Because these early fossils are spread across the continent, many researchers now favour a pan-African model. In this model our species emerged across a network of partly connected populations throughout Africa, rather than in a single "Garden of Eden". Genetic data agree. Living Africans hold more genetic diversity than the rest of the world combined, and the deepest branches of the human family tree are found in African populations.

The behavioural evidence also builds up in Africa. People were using red ochre by at least 300,000 years ago. Pierced shell beads appear from about 100,000 years ago, in North Africa and the Levant. At Blombos Cave, South Africa, there are engraved ochre pieces and a cross-hatched drawing on stone about 75,000–73,000 years old (Henshilwood et al., 2018).

3.2 Out of Africa, again

Homo sapiens left Africa more than once:

  • Early dispersals. A jaw from Misliya Cave, Israel (about 194,000–177,000 years ago), and burials at Skhul and Qafzeh (about 120,000–90,000 years ago) show our species in the Levant long before the main dispersal (Hershkovitz et al., 2018). These populations seem to have contributed little or nothing to living people.
  • The main dispersal: about 60,000–50,000 years ago. Genetic evidence shows that nearly all ancestry in living non-African people traces to a population that left Africa in this period. From South-West Asia, people spread rapidly. They reached Island Southeast Asia and Sahul (Module 4), Europe by about 45,000 years ago, and eventually the Americas.

Outside Africa, these people met others who were already there.

3.3 Neanderthals

Neanderthals lived in Europe and Western and Central Asia from about 400,000 until about 40,000 years ago. Fossils from Sima de los Huesos ("pit of the bones") in Spain, about 430,000 years old, already carry Neanderthal-type DNA. This makes them early members of the Neanderthal lineage (Meyer et al., 2016). They were stocky and powerfully built, with large noses, a projecting mid-face and, on average, slightly larger brains than recent humans.

The old image of brutish cave-dwellers is wrong. The evidence shows Neanderthals:

  • made sophisticated stone tools and birch-tar glue (which requires controlled heating);
  • hunted large game, and also ate plants, shellfish and birds;
  • buried at least some of their dead;
  • cared for injured and disabled people over long periods;
  • used pigments and eagle talons, probably as ornaments.

Some cave markings in Spain have been dated to before modern humans arrived and attributed to Neanderthals, though these claims are contested.

Neanderthals disappear from the record about 40,000 years ago, within several thousand years of modern humans arriving in Europe. They did not disappear completely, as the next section explains.

3.4 Reading ancient DNA

DNA breaks down after death, but in cold, stable conditions fragments can survive for hundreds of thousands of years. From the late 1990s, Svante Pääbo's group at the Max Planck Institute in Leipzig developed methods to recover these fragments and reject contamination from modern DNA. In 2010 they published a draft Neanderthal genome (Green et al., 2010). Pääbo received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

The Neanderthal genome showed something unexpected. People of non-African descent carry around 1–2% Neanderthal DNA, the result of interbreeding after modern humans left Africa. Recent studies place the main period of mixing at roughly 50,000–45,000 years ago (Iasi et al., 2024). Some of these genes still affect people today, including variants linked to immune responses, skin and, in some cases, disease risk.

Case study

Denisovans: a human group discovered through DNA

First identified
2010, from DNA
Type site
Denisova Cave, Siberia
Known fossils
A handful: teeth, a finger bone, jaws, a skull
Legacy today
Highest in Papua New Guinea and Australia

A genome before a face. In 2010 Pääbo's team sequenced DNA from a tiny finger bone fragment found in Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of Siberia. It was neither modern human nor Neanderthal. It came from a previously unknown group, related to Neanderthals but separate from them for hundreds of thousands of years. They became known as Denisovans (Reich et al., 2010). This was the first time a human group was identified from its genome rather than its anatomy. In 2018 a bone from the same cave turned out to belong to a girl with a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father, direct proof that the two groups had children together (Slon et al., 2018).

Finding the bodies. For years Denisovans were known only from a few teeth and bone fragments. That has changed:

  • Xiahe jaw, Tibetan Plateau (about 160,000 years old): identified in 2019 using ancient proteins, which survive longer than DNA. Tibetans today carry a Denisovan-derived gene variant (EPAS1) that helps them live at high altitude (Chen et al., 2019; Huerta-Sánchez et al., 2014).
  • Tam Ngu Hao 2 cave, Laos (about 164,000–131,000 years old): a child's molar described in 2022 that most closely matches Denisovan teeth. Australian researchers were part of the dating team. It places Denisovans in tropical Southeast Asia (Demeter et al., 2022).
  • Harbin skull, north-east China (at least 146,000 years old): a near-complete cranium, described in 2025 as Denisovan on the basis of ancient proteins and mitochondrial DNA. It shows a large-brained, heavy-browed human with very large teeth (Fu et al., 2025).

Why it matters for our region. Denisovan ancestry is low in most of mainland Asia. It is highest in the people of New Guinea and in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, at roughly 3–5% of the genome according to most estimates. Genetic studies suggest at least two or three distinct Denisovan populations contributed DNA to the ancestors of people in Island Southeast Asia and Sahul (Jacobs et al., 2019). This means Denisovans, or a closely related group, probably lived in Island Southeast Asia, possibly east of the Wallace Line, when modern humans passed through on their way to Sahul.

Australian research

Where did the meeting happen? Researchers at the University of Adelaide's Australian Centre for Ancient DNA have analysed the genetic data against the fossil record. They argue that the patterns are best explained by modern humans meeting Denisovan-related groups in Wallacea, the island zone covered in Module 2, on the way to Sahul. They also found no clear genetic signal of interbreeding with H. floresiensis or H. luzonensis, though such signals would be hard to detect (Teixeira et al., 2021). The search for Denisovan fossils east of the Wallace Line is now a major goal for Australian and Indonesian teams.

Perspective

Genetic research involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples has a difficult history. Samples were sometimes taken without meaningful consent and used in ways communities did not agree to. Current studies, such as those coordinated through the National Centre for Indigenous Genomics at the ANU, operate under Indigenous governance, with communities deciding how samples and data are used. Many First Nations people also emphasise that their own knowledge holds they have always been on Country, and that genetic findings are one source of knowledge, not the final word on identity.

        3.5 Minds made visible

        When did humans start making art? For most of the 20th century the answer centred on Europe. The painted caves of France and Spain, such as Chauvet (about 36,000 years old) and Lascaux (about 17,000 years old), suggested a "creative explosion" among the first modern Europeans. Discoveries in Indonesia have largely overturned that view.

        Case study · Australian research

        The cave art of Sulawesi and Borneo

        Region
        Maros-Pangkep karst, South Sulawesi
        Partners
        Griffith University (ARCHE) and Indonesian research agencies
        Oldest dated
        Painting at least 51,200 years old
        Method
        Laser-ablation uranium-series dating

        The limestone caves of the Maros-Pangkep region contain hundreds of hand stencils and paintings of animals, especially the warty pig and the dwarf buffalo (anoa) that are found only on Sulawesi. Local researchers had long recorded them, but assumed they were no more than about 10,000 years old.

        Dating the art. Researchers associated with Griffith University's Australian Research Centre for Human Evolution, working with Indonesian colleagues including Adhi Agus Oktaviana and with archaeologists Maxime Aubert and Adam Brumm, adapted a method to date it. Water seeping over cave walls leaves thin crusts of calcite, sometimes forming over the paint. Calcite contains traces of uranium, which decays into thorium at a known rate. By measuring the uranium–thorium ratio in the crust directly above the pigment, the team obtains a minimum age: the painting must be older than the crust that formed over it.

        Results.

        • 2014: a hand stencil in Sulawesi was dated to at least 39,900 years, as old as the oldest European cave art (Aubert et al., 2014).
        • 2018: a painting of an animal in East Kalimantan, Borneo, was dated to at least 40,000 years (Aubert et al., 2018).
        • 2019: a painted scene at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, at least 43,900 years old, shows small figures with some animal features hunting pigs and anoa. It is one of the oldest known depictions of a story (Aubert et al., 2019).
        • 2024: at Leang Karampuang, a scene of three human-like figures interacting with a pig was dated to at least 51,200 years. Dated with an improved laser method, it was, at publication, the oldest securely dated figurative art in the world (Oktaviana et al., 2024).

        Why it matters. Figurative art and storytelling were present at the eastern edge of Asia earlier than in Europe, and close in time to the first settlement of Sahul. It suggests the ability to make art was part of the cultural toolkit modern humans carried out of Africa, not a later European invention. The people who made these paintings were likely part of the same broad movement of people who went on to reach Australia. Australia itself holds some of the world's largest and longest-lived rock art traditions, which are covered in Module 4.

        How we know: minimum ages and the limits of radiocarbon

        • Radiocarbon dating needs organic carbon and only reaches back about 50,000 years. Many rock paintings use mineral pigments such as ochre, which contain no carbon to date.
        • Uranium-series dating of overlying calcite gives a minimum age, not the age of the painting itself. The painting could be much older.
        • Every new, older date pushes the record back, but it is still a floor rather than a ceiling. This is why researchers say "at least".

        Activity · Hotspot

        How a painting gets a minimum age

        ✓ Completed

        This cross-section shows a painted cave wall in Sulawesi. Work through how the date is obtained. Select each numbered point.

        Select a numbered point on the diagram to read about it.

        1. Limestone wall

        The cave rock. Water moving through limestone carries dissolved calcium carbonate, which later forms the calcite crust.

        2. Pigment layer

        The painting, usually red ochre or another mineral pigment. It contains no carbon, so it cannot be radiocarbon dated directly.

        3. Calcite crust

        Water seeping over the wall leaves thin calcite layers, like a coral crust, that build up over thousands of years. Calcite takes in traces of uranium but almost no thorium when it forms.

        4. Sampling point next to the pigment

        The layer touching the pigment is the oldest calcite. A laser removes microscopic amounts across the layers, and the ratio of uranium to its decay product, thorium, gives each layer’s age. The age of the innermost layer is the painting’s minimum age.

        5. Outer layers

        Each layer outwards should give a younger date. Checking that ages decrease in order away from the paint guards against contamination or uranium loss.

        6. The unknown gap

        We do not know how long the paint sat on the wall before calcite started to grow. That gap could be years or millennia, which is why researchers say "at least 51,200 years".

        3.6 Why only us?

        By about 40,000 years ago Neanderthals were gone. Denisovans may have survived somewhat later in parts of Asia. H. floresiensis and H. luzonensis disappear from the record about 50,000 years ago, around the time modern humans reached the region, though a direct link is not proven. Within a geologically short time, Homo sapiens was the only human species left. Explanations include:

        • Population size and networks. Modern human groups may have been larger and more connected, allowing ideas and innovations to spread and survive. Genetic evidence suggests late Neanderthal populations were small and inbred.
        • Absorption. Some groups were partly absorbed into modern human populations through interbreeding. Their genes did not all disappear.
        • Competition. Modern humans may have competed for the same resources, sometimes directly.
        • Climate. Rapid climate swings between about 60,000 and 30,000 years ago put small, isolated populations under stress.
        • Chance. Small populations can die out by bad luck.

        Most researchers think a combination of these was at work, and that the balance differed from region to region.

        Module summary

        • Homo sapiens emerged across Africa about 300,000 years ago. The oldest fossils are from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco.
        • Most ancestry of living non-Africans comes from a dispersal out of Africa about 60,000–50,000 years ago.
        • Ancient DNA shows interbreeding with Neanderthals (about 1–2% in non-Africans) and Denisovans (highest in Papuan and First Nations Australian populations).
        • Denisovans were identified from DNA in 2010. Fossils from Tibet, Laos and China now give them a physical form.
        • Cave art in Sulawesi, dated by Australian–Indonesian teams to at least 51,200 years, is the oldest known figurative art.
