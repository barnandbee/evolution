Reaching Sahul: the peopling of Australia

The first people to reach Australia made the earliest known planned sea voyages. Their descendants adapted to deserts, ice-age highlands, rising seas and the loss of the megafauna, and kept continuous cultures that are among the oldest in the world. This module covers that history and how it is researched today.

4.1 A continent called Sahul

For most of the past 100,000 years, sea levels were lower than today, at times by over 100 metres. Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania formed a single landmass that researchers call Sahul. It included large areas of now-submerged land: the Sahul Shelf off the Kimberley and Northern Territory, and a plain across what is now the Gulf of Carpentaria and Torres Strait.

As the last ice age ended, the seas rose. Tasmania was cut off by the flooding of Bass Strait around 12,000–14,000 years ago. New Guinea was separated by the flooding of Torres Strait about 8,000 years ago. Australia's present coastline is geologically recent.

4.2 The first voyages

As Module 2 explained, Sahul was never joined to Asia. Even at the lowest sea levels, getting from Sunda (the Asian continental shelf) to Sahul meant crossing Wallacea. That required a series of sea crossings, at least one of them probably 70–90 km or more, depending on the route. Researchers have modelled two main routes:

  • A northern route through Borneo, Sulawesi and the Moluccas to New Guinea.
  • A southern route through the Lesser Sunda islands to Timor, then across to the now-submerged Sahul Shelf off north-western Australia.

Australian research

Planned, not accidental. Researchers with the former ARC Centre of Excellence for Australian Biodiversity and Heritage (CABAH), a collaboration of Australian universities, modelled the islands, sea levels and currents of the time. Their work showed that the targets of many crossings were visible from high points on the departure island. Demographic modelling published in 2019 estimated that a population of at least about 1,300 people was needed to establish a population that would survive. This implies deliberate, repeated voyages by groups of men, women and children, not a few people drifting on a log. This makes the settlement of Sahul the earliest strong evidence of planned seafaring anywhere.

No watercraft from this period survive. Bamboo rafts are the most commonly proposed technology, and the people who made the crossings would have needed rope, planning, navigation skills and the cooperation to organise them.

4.3 How long ago?

Few questions in Australian archaeology have been argued over as much as the date of first arrival. Early estimates in the 1950s were about 10,000 years. Radiocarbon dating at Lake Mungo pushed that past 40,000 years in the 1970s. Today the debate is mainly between two positions:

  • A "short" chronology of about 50,000–47,000 years, based on the age at which radiocarbon dates cluster across many sites, and on some estimates from genetic data.
  • A "long" chronology of about 65,000 years or more, based mainly on luminescence dating at Madjedbebe in Arnhem Land.

Case study · Australian research

Madjedbebe, Mirarr Country

Location
Western Arnhem Land, NT
Traditional Owners
Mirarr people
Key publication
2017, led by University of Queensland
Claimed age
About 65,000 years (±5,700)

The site. Madjedbebe (formerly known as Malakunanja II) is a sandstone rock shelter on Mirarr Country, close to the Ranger uranium mine and surrounded by Kakadu National Park. It was first excavated in the 1970s and 1980s. A new excavation took place in 2012 and 2015 under a formal agreement between the Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation, which represents the Mirarr, and a team led by Chris Clarkson of the University of Queensland. The agreement gave the Mirarr control over the excavation and the right to stop work, and recognised their ownership of the finds.

The finds. The lowest dense layer of artefacts contained more than 10,000 stone artefacts, including:

  • grinding stones used for seeds and pigments, among the oldest known anywhere;
  • pieces of ground and used ochre, along with reflective mica-rich pigments;
  • fragments of edge-ground stone axes, the oldest known in the world at the time of publication.

Charred plant remains showed people eating fruits, nuts, seeds and starchy plants.

The date. The sediments were dated by optically stimulated luminescence (OSL). This measures when grains of quartz were last exposed to sunlight before being buried. The team dated thousands of individual grains, and the lowest artefact layer gave an age of about 65,000 years.

The debate. Some archaeologists argue that artefacts may have moved downwards through the sandy sediment, for example through termite activity, trampling or the decay of roots. If so, they could be younger than the sand around them. They point out that most other early Australian sites cluster at about 50,000 years or younger, and that some genetic estimates of the timing of arrival are also younger. The excavation team responded with further analysis showing that the artefacts are in distinct, ordered layers and that individual sand grains show little sign of mixing. The debate continues, and the date is best treated as strong but contested.

Why it matters. If the 65,000-year date is right, people reached Sahul earlier than the main dispersal out of Africa is usually dated, which would require rethinking parts of that timeline. Either way, Madjedbebe shows a sophisticated culture at or near the time of first arrival. Its excavation agreement is also widely regarded as a model of how research should be done in partnership with Traditional Owners.

1. Upper layers

The most recent deposits: shell, stone artefacts and charcoal from the last several thousand years, which radiocarbon can date. Many generations of Mirarr ancestors used this shelter.

2. OSL sample tubes

Tubes hammered into the section wall collect sand without exposing it to light. In the lab, optically stimulated luminescence measures when each quartz grain was last exposed to sunlight. The team measured thousands of individual grains.

3. Lowest dense artefact band

The first dense layer of occupation, dated to about 65,000 years ago. It contains more than 10,000 artefacts, including grinding stones, ground ochre, reflective pigments and fragments of edge-ground axes.

4. Sand with few artefacts

Below the dense band, artefacts are rare or absent. The excavators read this as the period before people arrived, with the occasional artefact having moved down from above.

5. Termite and root disturbance

The debate. Critics argue that termites, roots and trampling could move artefacts down through loose sand, making them look older than they are. The team responded that the artefacts sit in distinct, ordered layers and that individual sand grains show little sign of mixing.

Case study · Australian research

Lake Mungo and the Willandra Lakes

Location
South-western NSW
Traditional Owners
Paakantyi, Mutthi Mutthi and Ngiyampaa peoples
World Heritage
Inscribed 1981
Burials
About 42,000–40,000 years old

A landscape of dry lakes. The Willandra Lakes were once a chain of freshwater lakes fed by a creek from the Lachlan River. They held water through much of the last ice age and dried out about 18,000 years ago. On the eastern side of the lakes, wind built up crescent-shaped dunes known as "lunettes". The lunette at Lake Mungo, known as the Walls of China, is eroding and exposing a record of human life, climate and environment stretching back more than 45,000 years.

Mungo Lady. In 1968 geomorphologist Jim Bowler, then at the ANU, noticed burnt bones eroding from the lunette. They were the remains of a young woman who had been cremated; her bones were then broken and buried. Now dated to about 42,000–40,000 years ago, this is the oldest known cremation in the world.

Mungo Man. In 1974 Bowler found a second burial nearby. A man had been laid on his back with his hands crossed and his body sprinkled with red ochre that had been brought from at least 200 km away. The burial is of a similar age to Mungo Lady. It is one of the oldest known uses of ochre in a burial anywhere.

Why the discoveries mattered. Mungo Lady and Mungo Man showed that people in inland Australia had complex beliefs and ceremonies about death more than 40,000 years ago, at a time when such evidence was scarce worldwide. They also became central to public recognition of the antiquity of Aboriginal presence in Australia.

Return to Country. The remains were taken to Canberra for study, without the consent of the Traditional Owners. Over the following decades, the Paakantyi, Mutthi Mutthi and Ngiyampaa peoples, led by Elders including Mutthi Mutthi Elder Alice Kelly, campaigned for the ancestors' return.

  • 1992: Mungo Lady was handed back to Traditional Owners.
  • November 2017: Mungo Man and more than 100 other ancestors were returned to Country in a ceremony at Lake Mungo. Jim Bowler publicly supported the return.

Decisions about the ancestors' final resting place are led by the Traditional Owners.

Footprints. In 2003 a young Mutthi Mutthi woman, Mary Pappin Jr, found human footprints in a clay pan in the Willandra. Several hundred prints, about 20,000–23,000 years old, record adults, children and teenagers walking and running across wet ground around the time of the last glacial maximum. They are one of the largest collections of ice-age human footprints in the world. They were recorded jointly by researchers and Traditional Owners, including Aboriginal rangers trained in tracking.

4.4 A changing continent

The megafauna

When people arrived, Sahul was home to giant animals:

  • Diprotodon, a wombat relative the size of a rhinoceros;
  • Thylacoleo, the "marsupial lion";
  • Procoptodon, a short-faced kangaroo about 2 metres tall;
  • Genyornis, a large flightless bird;
  • giant monitor lizards and other large species.

Most of these species were extinct by about 40,000 years ago. Why remains one of the most contested questions in Australian science. The main explanations are:

  • Human impact. Hunting of slow-breeding animals, and changes to vegetation through landscape burning. Supporters point to extinctions following human arrival across the world.
  • Climate. Increasing aridity and less reliable water through the late Pleistocene.
  • Both. Many recent studies favour combined pressure that differed from region to region. In some areas, people and megafauna appear to have coexisted for thousands of years.

Direct evidence of hunting is rare. Proposed examples, such as burnt eggshell thought to be from Genyornis, have themselves been debated.

Fire on Country

In 1969 archaeologist Rhys Jones described Aboriginal landscape burning as "fire-stick farming". Regular, low-intensity, patchy burning cleared undergrowth, encouraged new grass growth that attracted game, made travel easier and reduced the risk of large, destructive fires. Records of charcoal in sediments suggest fire regimes changed at different times in different regions after people arrived. Today, cultural burning programs led by Traditional Owners, such as the savanna burning programs across northern Australia, are recognised for reducing wildfire risk and emissions.

Rising seas, remembered

When seas rose after about 18,000 years ago, Sahul lost millions of square kilometres of land. Aboriginal oral traditions from many coastal places describe a time when the coast lay further out and islands were part of the mainland. Researchers including geographer Patrick Nunn and linguist Nicholas Reid have argued that some of these traditions may preserve accurate memories of sea-level rise from more than 7,000 years ago. If so, they would be among the oldest accurately transmitted oral histories in the world.

Innovation over time

The record does not show an unchanging culture. Over tens of thousands of years, people in different regions developed new technologies and ways of living, including:

  • Rock art. Rock art in Arnhem Land and the Kimberley shows changing styles over many thousands of years. A painting of a kangaroo in the Kimberley has been dated to at least 17,000 years old, using radiocarbon dating of ancient mud wasp nests under and over the paint. The research was a partnership with the Balanggarra Aboriginal Corporation.
  • Seed grinding, which made arid landscapes productive.
  • Engineered aquaculture. At Budj Bim in western Victoria, the Gunditjmara people built channels, weirs and ponds in a lava landscape to trap and farm short-finned eels. Parts of the system are at least 6,600 years old. It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019, the first Australian site listed only for its Aboriginal cultural values.

4.5 Research ethics now

For much of the 20th century, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander remains and cultural material were collected, often without consent, and sent to museums and universities in Australia and overseas. Since the 1980s, research practice has changed substantially:

  • Repatriation. Australian governments and institutions now support the return of ancestral remains from collections in Australia and overseas. Examples include the return of the Kow Swamp remains in 1990 and of Mungo Man in 2017. Thousands of ancestors have been returned from overseas institutions.
  • The AIATSIS Code of Ethics (2020). The Australian Institute of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies Code of Ethics sets four principles for research with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples: Indigenous self-determination, Indigenous leadership, impact and value, and sustainability and accountability.
  • Research agreements. As at Madjedbebe, Traditional Owners increasingly set the terms of research, approve publications, and hold ownership of cultural material and data.
  • Indigenous data sovereignty. The right of Indigenous peoples to govern data about their communities, lands and ancestors. It is especially important in genetic research.

Key idea: Juukan Gorge

In May 2020, two rock shelters in Juukan Gorge in Western Australia's Pilbara were destroyed during iron-ore mining operations, with legal approval. Excavations had shown people had used them for about 46,000 years. The destruction happened despite the objections of the Traditional Owners, the Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura peoples. It led to a parliamentary inquiry, the resignation of senior executives at the mining company, and review of cultural heritage laws. The case showed that scientific significance alone did not protect a site when heritage law gave Traditional Owners little say.

            4.6 Open questions

            To close the course, here are some of the questions that researchers in Australia and our region are working on now:

            • When exactly did people first reach Sahul: closer to 65,000 or to 50,000 years ago?
            • Who were the Denisovans who met the ancestors of Papuans and First Nations Australians, and did they live east of the Wallace Line?
            • Did Homo floresiensis, H. luzonensis or other island hominins ever meet modern humans?
            • Which early hominins first crossed the sea to Sulawesi and Flores more than a million years ago, and how?
            • How did changing climate and people together shape the loss of the megafauna?
            • How can science and Indigenous knowledge work together on these questions, on terms set by communities?

            Many of the answers will come from sites in Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, the Philippines and Australia, and from partnerships between researchers and communities across the region.

            Module summary

            • Reaching Sahul required several sea crossings. It is the earliest strong evidence of planned seafaring.
            • People arrived at least about 50,000 years ago, and possibly about 65,000 years ago, based on evidence from Madjedbebe that is still debated.
            • The Lake Mungo burials, about 42,000 years old, show complex funeral practices. The return of Mungo Lady (1992) and Mungo Man (2017) is a landmark in repatriation.
            • Megafauna extinction probably resulted from a combination of human impact and climate, varying by region.
            • Research is now governed by principles of Indigenous self-determination and data sovereignty.

            Reflection

            The Madjedbebe agreement gave Traditional Owners the right to stop the research. Juukan Gorge showed what can happen when communities have no such power. Think about decisions in your own work that affect people with little formal say. How could their consent and control be built in from the start?
