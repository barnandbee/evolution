Genus Homo: tools, fire and the first journeys

Our own genus appears in Africa almost 3 million years ago. Within a million years, Homo had a body much like ours and had spread across Asia. Some lineages crossed open sea to islands where they evolved in unexpected directions.

2.1 The first Homo

The oldest fossil assigned to Homo is a partial lower jaw from Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia, dated to about 2.8 million years ago. Its teeth are smaller and its jaw more lightly built than an australopith's. Later, between about 2.4 and 1.5 million years ago, East Africa was home to Homo habilis ("handy man", named in 1964) and the larger-brained Homo rudolfensis. Whether these are really two species, and whether both belong in Homo, is still argued.

What changed? The trend is towards smaller teeth and jaws, and somewhat larger brains: around 500–700 cubic centimetres in early Homo, compared with 400–550 in australopiths. Cut-marked animal bones and Oldowan stone tools (simple sharp flakes struck from cobbles) show that meat and marrow were becoming a bigger part of the diet. Meat and marrow are dense in energy and fat. A larger brain is metabolically expensive: in modern humans it takes about 20% of resting energy while making up about 2% of body weight. Many researchers link these trends, though the direction of cause and effect is debated.

2.2 Homo erectus: a new body plan

From about 2 million years ago a new kind of hominin appears: Homo erectus (some researchers call the early African form Homo ergaster). The most complete skeleton is the "Turkana Boy" from Kenya, about 1.5 million years old, a youth who would probably have reached about 1.6–1.8 metres as an adult.

Compared with earlier hominins, H. erectus had:

  • Human-like body proportions: long legs, shorter arms, and a barrel-shaped rather than funnel-shaped chest. Climbing adaptations were largely gone.
  • A body built for endurance: features of the foot, Achilles tendon, inner ear and buttock muscles suggest walking and running long distances. Combined with probable sweating and reduced body hair, this may have allowed hunting in the heat of the day.
  • A bigger brain: from about 600 cubic centimetres in early forms to over 1,100 in later Asian populations.
  • A new technology: from about 1.76 million years ago, the Acheulean handaxe, a large teardrop-shaped tool worked on both faces to a planned shape. It is the first evidence of hominins imposing a mental template on stone. The basic design stayed in use for more than a million years.

Homo erectus was the longest-lived human species we know of. It spanned almost 2 million years and ranged from southern Africa to China and Java.

2.3 Fire and cooking

Fire leaves fragile traces. Charcoal blows away and burnt patches can be natural, so the timing of its control is uncertain.

  • Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa: burnt bone and plant ash deep inside a cave, about 1 million years ago. This is some of the earliest strong evidence of fire in a hominin context.
  • Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, Israel: burnt seeds, wood and flint clustered in hearth-like patterns, about 780,000 years ago.
  • Regular, habitual fire use becomes widespread at sites across Africa, Europe and Western Asia after about 400,000 years ago.

The cooking hypothesis argues that cooking made food softer and easier to digest, releasing more energy. This would have allowed smaller guts and teeth and supported larger brains. It fits the anatomical trend in H. erectus, but it relies on fire use starting earlier than the firm evidence currently shows. Fire also gave warmth, protection from predators, light that extended the social day, and a way to manage landscapes. That last point becomes important in Australia (Module 4).

2.4 Leaving Africa

For much of the 20th century, the first hominin dispersal out of Africa was attributed to large-brained, handaxe-making H. erectus about 1 million years ago. The evidence now points earlier and to less specialised hominins.

  • Dmanisi, Georgia (about 1.8 million years ago). Five skulls of small-brained (about 550–750 cubic centimetres) early Homo, with simple Oldowan-style tools, not handaxes. One individual had lost all but one tooth years before death and survived, which some researchers interpret as a sign of care by others.
  • Shangchen, China (about 2.1 million years ago). Stone tools in wind-blown loess deposits, dated by palaeomagnetism, older than any hominin fossil outside Africa.
  • Java, Indonesia. H. erectus fossils at Sangiran date to about 1.5 million years ago or earlier. Java was joined to mainland Asia by dry land whenever sea levels fell.

Australian research

The last Homo erectus. In the 1930s, 12 skull caps and two shin bones of late H. erectus were excavated at Ngandong on Java's Solo River. Their age was argued over for 80 years, with estimates ranging from about 550,000 to 27,000 years. In 2020 an international team, including researchers from Macquarie University and other Australian institutions, combined several dating methods across the site and surrounding landscape. They concluded that the fossils are about 117,000–108,000 years old. This makes Ngandong the last known appearance of H. erectus anywhere. The population seems to have vanished as open woodland gave way to rainforest.

2.5 Across the Wallace Line

In 1859 the naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace noticed a sharp boundary in the animals of the Indonesian archipelago. West of a line running between Bali and Lombok, and between Borneo and Sulawesi, the animals are Asian: tigers, rhinos, monkeys. East of it, they are increasingly Australian. The reason is geological. During ice ages, sea levels fell by up to about 120 metres, joining Java, Sumatra and Borneo to mainland Asia (the landmass called Sunda). Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania joined to form Sahul. Between them lies Wallacea, a zone of islands separated by deep-water straits that never dried out.

Any land mammal reaching Wallacea had to cross open water. For large animals, this is rare: elephant relatives (Stegodon) are strong swimmers, and rats and monkeys may have arrived on floating vegetation. Hominins did reach these islands, and very early:

  • Flores: stone tools at Wolo Sege at least 1 million years ago.
  • Sulawesi: stone tools at Calio dated to at least 1.04 million years ago (reported 2025), and tools at Talepu older than 118,000 years.
  • Luzon, Philippines: a butchered rhinoceros skeleton at Kalinga dated to about 709,000 years ago.

Whether these crossings were deliberate, or the result of hominins being swept out to sea by tsunamis or floods, is unknown. These islands also produced some of the most unusual hominins ever found.

Case study · Australian research

Liang Bua and Homo floresiensis

Site
Liang Bua cave, Flores, Indonesia
Discovered
2003; published 2004
Team
Indonesian–Australian
Age (revised)
Bones ~100–60 ka; tools ~190–50 ka

The find. In 2003 a joint team from Indonesia's National Research Centre for Archaeology and Australian universities, including the University of New England and later the University of Wollongong, was digging at Liang Bua, a large limestone cave on Flores. The project was led by the late Mike Morwood and R. P. Soejono, with Thomas Sutikna and Wahyu Saptomo directing the excavations. Six metres down they found a partial skeleton (LB1) of an adult who stood about 1.06 metres tall and had a brain of about 420 cubic centimetres, the size of a chimpanzee's. In 2004 the team named a new species, Homo floresiensis. The media nicknamed it "the hobbit".

The controversy. A brain this small in a human relative living alongside Homo sapiens seemed impossible to some researchers. They argued that LB1 was a modern human with a disorder such as microcephaly, Down syndrome or a thyroid condition. In 2004–05 the bones were taken out of the excavating team's custody for several months and some were damaged, and access to the cave was blocked for a time afterwards. The response came from detailed anatomy. Remains of more individuals were recovered, and studies of LB1's wrist, feet, shoulder and jaw showed primitive features found in early hominins, not in any modern human condition. Most researchers now accept H. floresiensis as a distinct species.

The dates were wrong. The first publications put the skeletons at about 18,000 years old, which would have meant tiny hominins survived long after modern humans reached the region. Continued excavation showed that the cave's sediment layers had been misread: the skeletons lay in older deposits cut through by a later erosion surface. In 2016 the team published revised ages. The skeletal remains date to about 100,000–60,000 years ago, and stone tools made by H. floresiensis range from about 190,000 to 50,000 years ago. The team corrected their own headline result in print, which is how science is meant to work.

Deep island history. At Mata Menge, about 70 km east, a jaw fragment and teeth published in 2016 showed that small-bodied hominins were on Flores about 700,000 years ago. An arm-bone fragment reported in 2024 suggests they were even smaller than LB1. The two leading explanations are:

  • Island dwarfing: H. erectus reached Flores and shrank over hundreds of thousands of years in an environment with limited food and no large predators. The same happened to Stegodon elephants on the island.
  • An older small-bodied ancestor: a hominin more like H. habilis left Africa early and reached Flores. Some features of the wrist and foot fit this explanation, but there is no fossil trail between them.

The discovery of Homo luzonensis in Callao Cave, Philippines (named in 2019, at least 50,000 years old) showed that Flores was not a one-off. Isolated islands produced their own distinct hominin lineages more than once.

How we know: lessons from Liang Bua

  • Stratigraphy comes first. A date only applies to a fossil if you are certain the fossil and the dated material came from the same layer and were not mixed.
  • Extraordinary claims need multiple lines of evidence. The case for a new species rested on many skeletal parts and individuals, not one skull.
  • Correction is normal. Major revisions to the date and interpretation of a famous find are a sign of a working field.

2.6 A crowded world

By 300,000 years ago the hominin world was diverse. H. erectus survived in Java, H. floresiensis lived on Flores, and the small-brained Homo naledi lived in southern Africa (fossils from the Rising Star cave system dated to about 335,000–236,000 years ago). Larger-brained populations in Africa and Eurasia were evolving into Neanderthals, Denisovans and, in Africa, our own species. Module 3 picks up their story.

Module summary

  • The Homo lineage dates to at least 2.8 million years ago, with smaller teeth and jaws and more meat in the diet.
  • Homo erectus had a modern body plan, endurance adaptations and the Acheulean handaxe, and lived for nearly 2 million years.
  • Fire was used by about 1 million years ago, but became routine only after about 400,000 years ago.
  • Hominins left Africa by about 2 million years ago, before large brains or handaxes.
  • Hominins crossed the sea into Wallacea by about 1 million years ago. Isolated islands produced the small-bodied H. floresiensis and H. luzonensis.
