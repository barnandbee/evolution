Deep roots: from apes to the first hominins

The human lineage began in Africa between roughly 7 and 2 million years ago. This module covers what separates hominins from other apes, why upright walking came long before big brains, and how we put dates on bones millions of years old.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Explain what humans share with other primates and where our lineage separated from the chimpanzee lineage.
  2. List the traits palaeoanthropologists use to identify a fossil as a hominin.
  3. Describe the main early hominin groups, including Ardipithecus, Australopithecus and Paranthropus.
  4. Explain why upright walking evolved millions of years before large brains.
  5. Outline how fossils in East and South Africa are dated.

1.1 Family resemblance

Humans are primates, and more specifically great apes. Our closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos. Gorillas are the next closest, then orangutans. Across the parts of the genome that can be directly compared, human and chimpanzee DNA differs by only about 1.2% at the level of single DNA letters, although the difference is larger once insertions, deletions and duplications are counted.

A common misconception is that humans evolved from chimpanzees. We did not. Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor that lived in Africa. Estimates from genetic data for when the two lineages separated mostly fall between about 6 and 10 million years ago, depending on the assumed mutation rate and generation time. Since then, both lineages have kept evolving. A modern chimpanzee has had exactly as long to change as we have.

From our primate ancestry we inherit grasping hands with nails rather than claws, forward-facing eyes with depth perception and colour vision, large brains for our body size, slow growth with a long childhood, and complex social lives. Human evolution builds on this primate base. It does not start from scratch.

Australian research

Not every ape lineage survived. Gigantopithecus blacki, a giant ape from southern China, may have stood about 3 metres tall. In 2024 a Chinese–Australian team, with dating led from Macquarie University and tooth-chemistry analysis from Southern Cross University, showed it went extinct between about 295,000 and 215,000 years ago. As forests became more seasonal, it failed to shift its diet, while orangutans in the same region adapted. Specialisation can be fatal when environments change, and this is a theme that recurs across hominin evolution.

1.2 What makes a hominin

Hominin is the term for humans and every extinct species more closely related to us than to chimpanzees. Identifying an early hominin is hard, because near the split the two lineages looked very similar. Researchers look for a cluster of traits:

Upright walking (bipedalism)A forward-placed foramen magnum (the hole where the spinal cord exits the skull); a short, bowl-shaped pelvis; angled thigh bones; an arched footThe earliest and most reliable hominin signal
Smaller canine teethCanines that do not project and are not sharpened against the lower premolarMay reflect changes in competition between males
Thick tooth enamel, large molarsEnamel thickness and grinding surfaceDiets with tougher, more abrasive foods
Larger brainsCranial capacity relative to body sizeA much later development, mainly within Homo

Key idea

Human traits did not appear together as a package. Upright walking came first, roughly 6–7 million years ago. Significant brain expansion followed more than 4 million years later. Scientists call this mosaic evolution: different parts of the body evolve at different rates.

1.3 The earliest candidates

Three groups dating to between about 7 and 4.4 million years ago are the main candidates for the earliest hominins. All are known from limited material, and each claim has been challenged.

  • Sahelanthropus tchadensis (about 7 million years ago, Chad). It is known from a distorted cranium nicknamed Toumaï, found in 2001. Its discovery 2,500 km west of the East African Rift showed that early hominins were not confined to East Africa. Whether it walked upright is still argued over limb bones found nearby.
  • Orrorin tugenensis (about 6 million years ago, Kenya). The shape of its thigh bones suggests it bore weight on two legs.
  • Ardipithecus (5.8–4.4 million years ago, Ethiopia). A partial skeleton of Ar. ramidus known as "Ardi", dated to 4.4 million years ago, was published in detail in 2009. Ardi had a pelvis partly adapted for upright walking but kept an opposable big toe for grasping branches. Plant and animal fossils around Ardi indicate woodland, not open grassland.

Ardi changed thinking in two ways. First, it argued against the idea that our ancestors went through a chimpanzee-like knuckle-walking stage. Second, it challenged the "savannah hypothesis", the long-held view that hominins stood up because forests gave way to open grassland.

1.4 Walking first

Why walk upright? Bipedalism has costs. It is slower than four-legged running, it strains the lower back and knees, and it narrows the birth canal. It must have offered advantages strong enough to outweigh these. The main hypotheses, which are not mutually exclusive, are:

  • Energy efficiency. Walking on two legs uses less energy than chimpanzee-style knuckle-walking over long distances between patches of food.
  • Carrying. Freed hands can carry food, infants or tools.
  • Feeding posture. Standing to reach fruit in low branches, as some apes do today.
  • Heat management. An upright body exposes less surface to the midday sun and catches more breeze. This matters more in open landscapes.

The clearest evidence of early upright walking is the Laetoli footprints in Tanzania. About 3.66 million years ago, at least two individuals walked across damp volcanic ash, which then hardened. The prints show a heel strike and a big toe aligned with the other toes. These are the features of a human-like stride. A second set of prints at Laetoli, re-examined in 2021, may belong to a different hominin with a different gait. This suggests more than one way of walking upright existed at the same time.

1.5 The australopiths

Between about 4.2 and 2 million years ago, Africa was home to several species of Australopithecus ("southern ape"). They walked upright on the ground but kept long arms and curved fingers suited to climbing. Their brains were about 400–550 cubic centimetres, only slightly larger than a chimpanzee's. They stood between about 1.0 and 1.5 metres tall.

  • Au. anamensis (4.2–3.8 million years ago) is the earliest. A near-complete cranium described in 2019 showed it overlapped in time with the next species.
  • Au. afarensis (3.9–2.9 million years ago) includes "Lucy", a 40% complete skeleton found at Hadar, Ethiopia, in 1974 and dated to about 3.2 million years ago. More than 300 individuals of this species are known, making it one of the best-understood early hominins.
  • Au. africanus (about 3.3–2.1 million years ago) is from South African caves. It was the first australopith ever named, as the case study below describes.
  • Au. sediba (about 1.98 million years ago, Malapa, South Africa) combines australopith traits with some features seen in Homo, such as a more human-like hand.

Australian research

Reading a childhood in tooth chemistry. Teeth grow in daily layers, much like tree rings. In 2019 a team led by Renaud Joannes-Boyau of Southern Cross University used lasers to map trace elements along the growth layers of 2.6–2.1-million-year-old Au. africanus teeth. Regular cycles of barium and lithium showed that infants were breastfed intensively for about their first year, then returned to breast milk during seasonal food shortages over several years. This is direct evidence of the stress early hominins faced, without any assumptions about behaviour.

Case study

The Taung Child and Raymond Dart

Found
1924, Taung, South Africa
Age
About 2.5–2.8 million years
Species
Australopithecus africanus
Described by
Raymond Dart (born Brisbane)

In 1924 quarry workers at Taung sent a block of limestone to Raymond Dart, a young Australian anatomist who had trained at the University of Queensland and the University of Sydney and was then teaching in Johannesburg. Dart spent weeks freeing a small face, jaw and natural brain cast from the rock. It belonged to a child of about three years old.

In Nature in 1925 Dart named a new species, Australopithecus africanus. He made two claims. First, the foramen magnum sat far enough forward to indicate upright walking. Second, Africa, not Asia or Europe, was where humans originated.

The British scientific establishment largely rejected both claims. There were three reasons:

  • Expectation. Most experts expected the human lineage to have evolved a large brain first. The Taung Child had a small brain.
  • Piltdown Man. A skull "found" in England in 1912 combined a human-like cranium with an ape-like jaw, which matched the brain-first expectation. It was exposed as a forgery in 1953: a medieval human skull with an orangutan jaw.
  • Prejudice. There was reluctance to accept Africa as humanity's birthplace, and doubt that a child's skull reported by a colonial outsider could be significant.

Further finds from South African caves in the 1930s and 1940s, especially by Robert Broom, eventually vindicated Dart. In 2006, analysis of damage to the Taung skull showed the child had probably been killed by a large bird of prey, an eagle.

Why it matters: it shows how strongly expectations can shape the interpretation of evidence, and that a forgery fitting those expectations can survive for 40 years. The same pattern, where data that do not fit the accepted model are dismissed until the evidence becomes overwhelming, appears again in Module 2 with Homo floresiensis.

1.6 A bush, not a ladder

The familiar "march of progress" image, a line of figures getting taller and more upright, is misleading. For most of the past 4 million years several hominin species lived at the same time, sometimes in the same landscape.

The robust australopiths, genus Paranthropus, are the clearest example. From about 2.7 to 1 million years ago they had huge molars, thick jaws and a bony crest on top of the skull to anchor powerful chewing muscles. They were a successful side branch, specialised for tough plant foods, and they lived alongside early Homo for over a million years before dying out.

Australian research

Three genera at once. In 2020 an international team led by Andy Herries of La Trobe University reported finds from Drimolen cave, South Africa, dated to about 2.04–1.95 million years ago. They included the oldest known skull of Homo erectus and an early Paranthropus robustus skull. With Au. sediba at nearby Malapa, this means three hominin genera, Australopithecus, Paranthropus and Homo, lived in the same small region at about the same time.

Who made the first stone tools? Tool-making was long treated as the defining trait of Homo ("Man the Toolmaker"). That view no longer holds:

  • Stone tools from Lomekwi 3, Kenya, reported in 2015, are about 3.3 million years old, older than any known Homo fossil.
  • At Nyayanga, Kenya, Oldowan tools about 2.6–3.0 million years old were found with Paranthropus teeth and with butchered hippopotamus bones (reported 2023).

Tool use probably has deep roots among hominins. Chimpanzees also use stone hammers, so this should not be a surprise.

1.7 Dating the deep past

A fossil without a date has little scientific value. No single method covers every site, so researchers combine several. Radiocarbon dating, the method most people know, only reaches back about 50,000 years. It is useless for the period covered in this module.

How we know: dating methods for the early hominin record

  • Argon–argon (⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar) dating. In East Africa's Rift Valley, fossils lie between layers of volcanic ash. Volcanic crystals contain potassium, which decays to argon at a known rate. Dating ash layers above and below a fossil brackets its age, often to within a few tens of thousands of years. Lucy, Ardi and the Laetoli footprints are all dated this way.
  • Palaeomagnetism. Earth's magnetic field has reversed many times. Magnetic minerals in sediments record the field's direction when they were laid down. Matching a sequence of reversals to the global timescale gives an age range.
  • Uranium–lead dating. South African cave fossils have no volcanic ash. Instead, layers of flowstone (calcite) above and below fossils can be dated by uranium decay to lead, as at Drimolen and Malapa.
  • Electron spin resonance (ESR) and uranium-series dating. These measure radiation damage and uranium uptake in tooth enamel. Australia has a strong tradition in these methods, including specialists at Griffith University's Australian Research Centre for Human Evolution and at the ANU.
  • Biochronology. Fossils of animals with well-known time ranges, such as particular pigs or elephants, act as a cross-check.

Strong dates come from several independent methods agreeing. When they disagree, it usually means something in the site's history has been misread, such as later disturbance mixing old and young material.

Module summary

  • Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor that lived in Africa roughly 6–10 million years ago.
  • Upright walking is the earliest hominin trait. Brain expansion came millions of years later.
  • Australopiths walked upright but kept climbing adaptations and small brains.
  • Hominin evolution was branching: several species often coexisted, and stone tools predate Homo.
  • Dates come from combining methods such as argon–argon dating of volcanic ash and uranium–lead dating of cave flowstones.

Reflection

The Taung Child was dismissed for more than 20 years because it did not match what experts expected. Think of a field you know well. What evidence might currently be discounted because it does not fit the accepted model, and what would it take to change minds?
